On 16 January 1958, as the Brazilian Navy ship Almirante Saldanha prepared to leave Trindade Island, a South Atlantic island some 600 miles from the Brazilian coast where the Navy ran a station for the International Geophysical Year, the civilian photographer Almiro Barauna, a guest of the Navy, took a sequence of photographs of a Saturn-shaped object passing over the island's peaks. Published in Rio on 21 February, they were soon among the most widely reproduced UFO photographs: accounts at the time said many of the crew had seen the object, and the Navy Minister was quoted saying the Navy held a secret it could not explain. The Navy Ministry itself said only that it had no reason to block their release and could not pronounce on the object, as the photographs were not sufficient proof.

The case against them is as strong as the case for them. The U.S. Naval Attaché found that neither the captain nor the executive officer had seen anything, that Barauna developed the film alone, and that he was known for trick photography; he judged a publicity stunt most likely, and Project Blue Book filed the photos as a hoax on that analysis without ever obtaining them. Menzel and Boyd found no Navy eyewitness on record, only two of the photographer's friends. In 2010 a family friend told Brazilian television that Barauna, by then dead, had admitted faking them with two spoons. On the other side, the Brazilian Navy's own report, as quoted by UFO researchers, says commercial photo experts found no sign of montage on the negatives while admitting a montage made beforehand could not be ruled out.

No official body has resolved it: the U.S. hoax verdict rests on a report written without the photographs, the Brazilian Navy never endorsed or rejected them, and the confession is second-hand. The case is marked partly explained.
