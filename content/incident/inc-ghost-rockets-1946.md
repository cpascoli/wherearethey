From late May 1946 the Swedish press reported "ghost rockets" — fast objects described as balls of fire with tails or shiny cigars — crossing southern Sweden, and soon Finland, Denmark and Norway. The Swedish Defence Staff asked the public for reports and received almost a thousand by the end of July; in August it imposed partial censorship on where sightings were made. Among the few trained observers was a Swedish Air Force pilot who on 14 August reported a wingless cigar-shaped object about 50 feet long over central Sweden.

The U.S. War Department's intelligence review of January 1947 found the evidence thin: no fragments beyond "nonmetallic slag", no physical evidence seen by U.S. personnel, and Swedish officials who privately blamed Soviet tests from Peenemünde while publicly moving toward a celestial explanation — a shift the review read as an effort to avoid trouble with the Soviet Union. It concluded that most sightings were meteors or fireworks, with perhaps a handful of genuine low-flying V-1-type missiles, probably Soviet tests of captured German technology.

The wave was the first post-war cluster of unexplained aerial reports to draw intelligence attention, a year before the 1947 American "flying saucer" wave; British scientific intelligence still cited "the Swedish incident" in 1952.
