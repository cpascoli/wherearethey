In early July 1947, rancher Mac Brazel found debris of tinfoil, paper, tape and sticks scattered across the Foster ranch near Corona, New Mexico. Maj. Jesse Marcel of Roswell Army Air Field collected it with the counter-intelligence agent Sheridan Cavitt, and on 8 July the base's public information officer, Walter Haut, put out a release saying it had recovered a "flying disc". Within hours Gen. Roger Ramey identified the material in Fort Worth as a weather balloon and radar target; the Star-Telegram quoted Marcel describing "the remnants of the kite and balloon", and the Roswell Daily Record printed Brazel's own account the next day.

The story lay dormant until 1978, when the ufologist Stanton Friedman found Marcel, who said the balloon story was a cover. In a long interview with the reporter Lee Speigel about 1980, held in this archive, Marcel described a debris field three quarters of a mile long and foil no sledgehammer could dent, and said he was convinced it was "not from anywhere on earth". Books from 1980 onwards added a crashed craft and recovered bodies, and later witnesses such as Lt. Col. Philip Corso made broader claims about recovered technology. Haut, asked on camera in 1990 whether he had seen any of the wreckage, answered "None whatsoever"; a sealed affidavit he signed in 2002, drafted by the researcher Donald Schmitt and published after his death, says he was shown an egg-shaped craft and two bodies. In October 2026 a redacted copy of that affidavit surfaced in the Pentagon's PURSUE release 07, as an appendix to a 2008 plan by the DIA's AAWSAP contractor; the release makes no finding on it.

In 1994 the U.S. Air Force acknowledged that the weather-balloon explanation had covered Project Mogul, a classified programme using balloon trains to detect Soviet nuclear tests, and traced the debris to Mogul flight 4 of 4 June 1947; Cavitt, the only living witness to the debris field, gave a sworn statement that he recognised a balloon. The 1997 report attributed the later accounts of "alien bodies" to parachute tests with dummies and two accidents of the 1950s. The GAO's 1995 review for Rep. Steven Schiff found no Roswell records at Wright Field and learned that the base's outgoing messages for 1946–49 had been destroyed without recorded authority. The researchers' case rests on testimony given thirty or more years after the event: Kevin Randle argues from Crary's diary that flight 4 was cancelled, and concedes there is no 1947 documentation of bodies; the Air Force's investigators found the same wreckage in every Fort Worth photograph.
