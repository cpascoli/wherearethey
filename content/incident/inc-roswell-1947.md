In early July 1947, rancher Mac Brazel found debris of tinfoil, paper, tape and sticks scattered across his ranch near Corona, New Mexico. Maj. Jesse Marcel of Roswell Army Air Field collected it, and on 8 July the base announced that it had recovered a "flying disc". Within hours Gen. Roger Ramey retracted the statement in Fort Worth and identified the material as a weather balloon.

The story lay dormant until 1978, when the ufologist Stanton Friedman interviewed Marcel, who said the balloon story was a cover. Books from 1980 onwards added accounts of a crashed craft and recovered bodies, and later witnesses such as Lt. Col. Philip Corso made broader claims about recovered technology.

In 1994 the U.S. Air Force acknowledged that the weather-balloon explanation had been a cover story for Project Mogul, a classified programme using balloon trains to detect Soviet nuclear tests, and traced the debris to a Mogul flight launched on 4 June 1947; its 1997 report attributed later accounts of "alien bodies" to parachute tests with dummies. A 1995 GAO review requested by Rep. Steven Schiff found no Roswell records at the CIA and no information on the alleged "Majestic 12" group. Most historians regard the crash-retrieval claims as unsupported.
