On 20 May 1967 Stefan Michalak, a Winnipeg industrial mechanic and weekend prospector, came out of the bush near Falcon Lake, Manitoba, with burns on his abdomen and said that two disc-shaped craft had come down, that one had landed, and that when he touched it a blast from its exhaust set his shirt alight. He was the only witness. A passing RCMP constable met him that afternoon, and the case was then investigated by the RCMP, the RCAF, the federal Department of Health and Welfare and the U.S. Air Force-funded Condon Committee.

The investigators could not find the site with him. A month later he said he had found it with an acquaintance and brought back soil, cloth and a tape that proved radioactive; a federal radiation expert found one small patch of radium-226 contamination on the rock, which his first tests suggested might be luminous paint, but left its origin undetermined. The RCMP listed discrepancies in his story and his removal of the evidence, but also burns, illness and a circle on the rock it could not explain; a National Defence summary says neither team could disprove his account.

The Condon Committee's investigator, Roy Craig, found the burns thermal, no radiation effects on Michalak, and so many inconsistencies that the report concluded the case offers no probative information about unconventional craft. The case remains Canada's best-documented close-encounter claim, and the 2025 Sky Canada report still lists it among the country's best-known cases. Michalak's account here is his own; no one else saw the objects.
