On the morning of 2 May 1957 (3 May in McDonald's account), two Askania cinetheodolite operators at Edwards Air Force Base, James Bittick and John Gettys, saw a golden, domed disc as they reached Askania Site 4 on the test range. They radioed their range director, Frank Baker, who told them to shoot manually since no other station was manned for triangulation, and they took a series of photographs as the object moved off. Base authorities took the photos, and the cameramen were told that day that they had seen a weather balloon distorted by the desert air, which they rejected. The case was reported in the Los Angeles Times and on the wire services that month and went to Project Blue Book as case 4715.

The Air Force's position, set out by the Edwards information office in June 1957, was that a weather balloon tracked with precision instruments passed at the time and bearings the men reported, and that the photos showed only small white specks. The physicist James McDonald interviewed both men and Baker and presented the case to the 1968 House symposium without that explanation; the researcher Brad Sparks says an analysis in the Blue Book file itself rejects the balloon. The case is best known through the astronaut Gordon Cooper, then a test pilot at Edwards, who from the late 1970s said he had sent the crew out, that they filmed a saucer landing on three legs and that the film vanished into the Pentagon. The witnesses describe no landing; Gettys told the sceptic James Oberg he did not think Cooper ever saw the object, Sparks says the crew had never heard of him, and Cooper himself said in 1978 that it was “all second hand evidence”. The photographs went to Blue Book, and two were published in 1976.
