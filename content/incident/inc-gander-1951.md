On the night of 10 February 1951 a U.S. Navy R5D transport flying from Keflavik, Iceland, to Argentia, Newfoundland, with two crews aboard, saw a glow on the sea that its pilots first took for a city. According to the Northeast Air Command intelligence report filed that day, the light grew very bright and large, appeared semicircular, closed on the aircraft, turned through 180 degrees and disappeared rapidly over the horizon. Five experienced North Atlantic aircrew, including the co-pilot Lt. Graham Bethune, signed reports; the weather was clear and Gander traffic control knew of no other aircraft.

The Air Force's intelligence center later filed it as the aurora borealis, and Philip Klass argued in 1974 that the crew had seen a reflection of the moon below the horizon. Bethune, who saw the explanation in 1990, rejected it, and in 2001 described the object rising from a circle of lights on the sea, pacing the aircraft and being tracked on radar; the radar tracking is not in the documents. The case papers, including the intelligence report and a Navy memo, are transcribed in Patrick Gross's casebook from National Archives copies.
