Before dawn on 20 September 1977, residents of Petrozavodsk and of a wide area of north-western Russia watched a vast light display for several minutes. A TASS report in *Izvestia* three days later described a huge star sending down sheaves of light, spreading over the city like a jellyfish and showering it with fine rays, then moving off towards Lake Onega; the head of the local weather observatory said his staff had never seen anything like it. It was also seen and photographed in Finland, and neighbouring countries asked Moscow whether it was a dangerous military experiment.

The case had institutional consequences. The Academy of Sciences told the Military-Industrial Commission that it could neither ignore nor explain such phenomena, and the commission's council met in October 1977. That meeting led to Setka, the Soviet state programme on anomalous phenomena that ran from 1978 to 1990.

The programme's own coordinators, physicist Yuli Platov and Col. Boris Sokolov, later identified the display as the launch of the satellite Kosmos-955 from Plesetsk. They tied some further effects to a failed ballistic-missile test launched in the same region at almost the same time. Jonathan McDowell's launch catalogue puts the Kosmos-955 launch at about 4 a.m. Moscow time that morning, the hour the TASS report gives. Platov and Sokolov explain such displays as sunlight scattered by a rocket's exhaust cloud above 100 km, seen by observers still in darkness, sometimes thousands of kilometres from the launch site.

*Sources: Platov & Sokolov, Vestnik RAN 70(6), 2000 (quoting the TASS report in Izvestia, 23 September 1977); Jonathan McDowell, GCAT launch list (entry 1977-091)*
