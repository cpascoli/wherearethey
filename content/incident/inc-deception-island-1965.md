In June and July 1965 personnel at the three bases on Deception Island in the South Shetlands, the Argentine Navy detachment, Chile's Air Force base Pedro Aguirre Cerda and Britain's Base B, reported moving coloured lights in the winter night sky. The peak came on 3 July, when the Chilean base and then almost the whole Argentine detachment, with three visiting Chilean NCOs, watched a luminous object for 20 minutes to an hour, and the Argentine station on Laurie Island (Orcadas) reported a fast light and a disturbance on its magnetometers. Both governments issued official communiqués, and the story went round the world's press.

The Argentine Navy said the object was lens-shaped and solid, and its detachment commander, Lt. Daniel Perissé, said it was neither a balloon, a star nor an aircraft. The Chilean base commander, Cdr. Mario Jahn Barrera, called it "something real". According to the 2012 re-investigation, the light was too faint to show on the Chilean photographs.

The magnetic evidence did not survive examination: the Condon study found the Orcadas trace within normal daily fluctuations, and the British base commander described the British report as a joke. The La Plata Observatory pointed to the Echo II satellite at the time. A 2012 re-investigation by private researchers matched the lights to a meteor, Echo II and Mars or bright stars, an explanation not endorsed by any official body.
