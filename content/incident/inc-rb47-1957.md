Before dawn on 17 July 1957 an RB-47H electronic-reconnaissance aircraft of the 55th Strategic Reconnaissance Wing from Forbes AFB, Kansas, commanded by Maj. Lewis D. Chase, was flying from the Gulf of Mexico back across Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas. Its ECM operators picked up an S-band radar signal that behaved like an airborne source; the pilot and co-pilot saw a brilliant light cross ahead of them, and later red lights northwest of Dallas–Fort Worth, which they chased. The wing's message says the ground radar site "Utah" (Duncanville) confirmed a target where the crew reported one, and lost it when they did. The signal followed the aircraft until it was abeam Oklahoma City.

Air Defense Command could offer no explanation and carried the case as unknown, and an Air Technical Intelligence Center analyst found it hard "to conclude that nothing was present". Yet Blue Book's record card marks it "Was Aircraft", citing American Airlines flight 655, the airliner in a separate near-miss near El Paso the same night. Ten years later the Condon study, given only the crew's recollections and told Blue Book had no file, called it unidentified. James McDonald then found the contemporary reports, and his 1971 article in *Astronautics & Aeronautics* presented it as unexplained.

Philip Klass answered in late 1971 that the signals came from the CPS-6B air-defence radars at Biloxi and Duncanville, the first light was a meteor, and the ground-radar target may have been an airliner. The pilot told Klass he accepted this. Klass's paper and the pilot's comment are on file, but they are a private analysis, not an Air Force finding.

*Sources: Project Blue Book case file "Forbes AFB, Kansas, July 1957" (NARA, T1206 roll 28): 55th SRW message, ADC and ATIC correspondence, record card, Klass's 1971 paper · [Condon Report, Case 5](https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case05.htm)*
