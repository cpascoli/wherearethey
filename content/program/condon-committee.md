The University of Colorado's UFO project, known after its scientific director, the physicist Edward U. Condon, was the U.S. Air Force's attempt, after the 1966 Michigan sightings and congressional hearing, to have its UFO files and new reports examined by independent scientists. The Air Force Office of Scientific Research contracted the university in the autumn of 1966, acting on the O'Brien committee's recommendation of university teams; work began that October and the final report, *Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects*, went to the Secretary of the Air Force on 31 October 1968.

The study ran field investigations of new reports, laboratory and photographic analyses and case studies of 59 reports, including older cases such as Lakenheath–Bentwaters (1956). It found most reports explainable, left some unexplained, and concluded that further extensive study was unlikely to advance science and that Blue Book need not continue; a National Academy of Sciences panel endorsed it in January 1969, and the Air Force closed Blue Book that December.

It was contested from inside and out. In February 1968 two of its scientists, David Saunders and Norman Levine, were dismissed; that spring Look magazine called the project a "flying saucer fiasco", NICAP broke with it and read out a memo attributed to its coordinator Robert Low about presenting the project as objective while expecting to find nothing, and Rep. J. Edward Roush questioned its objectivity on the House floor. Condon denied the charges. The often-repeated figure that about 30% of its cases stayed unexplained is not used here: it could not be checked against the report's case texts.
