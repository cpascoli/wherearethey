The Hessdalen lights are lights reported since late 1981 in the Hessdalen valley in central Norway, a 12 km valley of about 150 people south-east of Trondheim: white or yellow-white balls that hover for long periods near the ground or over the ridges, sometimes moving fast, and short white or blue flashes. Project Hessdalen was founded in June 1983 by a handful of Norwegian researchers led by the electrical engineer Erling Strand, because no official body was studying them. It is a private research project, not a government programme, though its first campaign drew on help from the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment and two universities.

Its first field campaign, from 21 January to 26 February 1984, ran radar, a spectrum analyser, a magnetometer, a laser, a seismograph, a geiger counter and cameras. It logged 188 sighting reports, only six of them with no likely known explanation, and 36 radar returns, three of which matched lights seen at the same moment. The technical report concludes that the phenomenon had not been identified, only measured. After the 1997 Pocantico workshop of the Sturrock panel, Strand and Bjørn Gitle Hauge, both then assistant professors at Østfold University College, set up a permanent automatic measurement station with video cameras watching the valley, and from 1998 Italian researchers (the EMBLA project) joined.

What the instruments recorded (radar returns, low-resolution spectra, photographs) is distinct from what has been inferred from them (speeds, luminous powers up to tens of kilowatts, chemical composition), and the inferences are disputed. A 2021 review finds no consensus; the physicist Matteo Leone identified at least one of the lights the EMBLA team photographed in 2002 as car headlights and rejected its power estimates.

*Sources: E. Strand, Project Hessdalen 1984: Final Technical Report (hessdalen.org) · G. Pascoli, Journal of Scientific Exploration 35(3), 2021 · M. Leone, Zeitschrift für Anomalistik 6, 2006*
