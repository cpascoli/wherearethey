Project Sign was the U.S. Air Force's first formal study of unidentified flying objects. It followed the summer 1947 wave of flying disc reports and the September 1947 opinion of Air Materiel Command, signed by Lt. Gen. Nathan Twining, that “the phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious”. The Technical Intelligence Division at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base opened it as project XS-304 on 22 January 1948, first under the name Project Saucer. Its consultants included the astronomer J. Allen Hynek of Ohio State University, who checked reports for astronomical explanations, psychologists of the Aero-Medical Laboratory, G. E. Valley of MIT and the Air Force's Scientific Advisory Board, and the Rand Project.

Its report of February 1949 reviewed 243 domestic and 30 foreign incidents, grouped as disks, cigar shapes, spheres and balls of light, and found no conclusive evidence either way: positive proof, it said, was unlikely “without examination of the remains of crashed objects”. Its appendices weighed whether the objects could be Soviet aircraft or visitors from other planets; Valley noted that a watching civilisation might be alarmed by atomic bombs, and the Rand study put interplanetary travel at a thousand to one against. The report recommended continuing at a minimum level, and Sign was succeeded by Project Grudge in 1949.

Edward Ruppelt, who later led Project Blue Book, wrote in 1956 that Sign had produced a top secret “Estimate of the Situation” concluding that UFOs were interplanetary, rejected by the Air Force chief of staff; no copy has surfaced, and the Air Force told Congress in 1966 that no such document existed. In 1949 Sign's officers also investigated the Caldwell disk-rotor aircraft, a 1930s experimental machine found abandoned near Baltimore, and judged it could not account for the reports.
