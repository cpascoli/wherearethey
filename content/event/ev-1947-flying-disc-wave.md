In the weeks after Kenneth Arnold's sighting of 24 June 1947, newspapers across the United States carried hundreds of reports of flying disks, peaking around the Fourth of July. They came from pilots, police officers and ordinary citizens, with clusters in the Pacific Northwest, and among them on 8 July was the Roswell Army Air Field press release announcing a recovered “flying disc”, withdrawn within hours as a weather balloon.

Some were photographed: in Tulsa, the Tulsa Daily World printed a freelance photographer's picture of eight lights, noting that other photographers said it could be faked by superimposing spots on a negative. Whether the wave was a real surge in sightings or a press-driven craze is still debated; the Air Force took it seriously enough that by September its Air Materiel Command judged the phenomenon “something real”, and Project Sign followed.

*Sources: Project Blue Book case files (U.S. National Archives) · Oregon Journal and Roswell Daily Record (July 1947) · Twining memo (1947)*
