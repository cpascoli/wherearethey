"Crash retrieval and reverse engineering" is the allegation that the United States, and possibly other nations, have recovered craft of non-human origin, in some accounts with their occupants, and run secret programmes to study and copy their technology. The modern story has a lineage. The FBI's 1950 Hottel memo relayed an informant's tale of three saucers recovered in New Mexico; Leonard Stringfield collected retrieval accounts from 1978 and said himself they could be considered hearsay; Bob Lazar's 1989 interviews with George Knapp, about working on recovered alien craft at a site called S-4 near Area 51, became the template for the genre, though his account is unverified and he is outside this archive's sourcing criteria; and Col. Philip Corso's 1997 book *The Day After Roswell* claimed he fed Roswell artifacts into industry, a claim the MoD was told rested on a record of unreliable testimony. The unverified Wilson–Davis notes of 2002, entered into a House record in 2022, describe a former DIA director refused access to a contractor's reverse-engineering programme.

Since 2023 the allegation has been made on the record by people who held security clearances. David Grusch testified under oath that he was told of a multi-decade crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering programme, that the U.S. "absolutely" holds UAP and that "biologics" came with some recoveries; Luis Elizondo answered yes under oath in 2024 to secret crash-retrieval programmes and to bodies; James Lacatski, who ran the DIA's AAWSAP, confirmed the DOPSR-cleared sentence in his book that the United States holds a craft of unknown origin and got inside it; the physicist Eric Davis told a 2025 congressional briefing that recovered craft are "not of this Earth"; Jake Barber says he flew a helicopter on a retrieval mission; and in October 2026 Jay Stratton, former head of the UAP Task Force, said he had seen non-human craft and beings "with my own eyes". None of them has produced a craft, a photograph or a document in public; Grusch says he gave witnesses and locations to the Inspector General and the intelligence committees. Lockheed Martin is the company named most often, in the Wilson–Davis notes, by Davis and in Knapp's account of Bigelow's negotiation for material. The alleged structure of the effort, the Disclosure Act's definition and the sworn testimony about it are filed under the [legacy program](/program/legacy-program).

The official answers are negative. In May 2022 Scott Bray, Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence, told Congress "we have no material"; Sean Kirkpatrick told the Senate in April 2023 that AARO had found "no credible evidence" of off-world technology, which Grusch called "not accurate"; AARO's 2024 historical report found "no empirical evidence" of reverse-engineering of extraterrestrial technology by the government or private companies, and said the executives of the companies named by interviewees denied it on the record; and Rep. Glenn Grothman said in November 2024 that the inspectors general and AARO had not substantiated Grusch's claims. Chris Mellon, who first brought crash-retrieval sources to Congress, warns that if recoveries are never confirmed the unproven allegation could discredit the UAP issue itself.
