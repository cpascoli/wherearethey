Boeing is one of the largest U.S. aerospace and defence manufacturers. The file also stands for McDonnell Douglas, the airframe and missile maker now part of Boeing, where the physicist Robert M. Wood spent 43 years and, by his account, ran a small company-funded study of how UFOs might be propelled in the late 1960s.

Boeing's engineers were central to the investigation of the 1967 Malmstrom shutdown. The 341st Strategic Missile Wing histories note that Boeing modification teams were at three other sites on 16 March 1967 but arrived after Echo Flight's ten missiles went No-Go, that Boeing engineers questioned the crews and joined the task group, and that tests at Boeing's Seattle plant reproduced the shutdown with a pulse on the logic coupler, which the Air Force's Ogden depot judged an electromagnetic or electrostatic fault peculiar to the Wing I system. Panellists at the 2013 Citizen Hearing cite the same Boeing bench test as evidence that whatever shut the missiles down worked through the coupler, and read the EMP filters installed afterwards as a response to Echo and Oscar Flight; the histories themselves say the UFO rumours were disproven. In 2003 Boeing contractors at Vandenberg Air Force Base were the first to report the "Red Square" over two missile-defence sites, as Ryan Graves and Jeffrey Nuccetelli told Congress.

In the recovered-technology allegations Boeing is named, not documented. Luis Elizondo lists it and McDonnell Douglas among the "Legacy Program" contractors in his memoir; Jay Stratton says he took unexplained cases to Boeing's Phantom Works and Lockheed's Skunk Works to rule out U.S. programs, and was sometimes told they could not discuss their own research. AARO reports that the executives of the companies named to it denied on the record ever holding or reverse-engineering extraterrestrial technology, and offers the Boeing–Lockheed–DARPA Dark Star drone as one of the secret aircraft mistaken for a flying saucer.
