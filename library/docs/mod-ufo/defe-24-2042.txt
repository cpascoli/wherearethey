I am writing concerning the message you left on our answersphone on the 15" July regarding the
1999 RAF Cosford Open Day Air Show and whether an airmiss was reported by the pilots of the
International team whose displays were affected.

An airmiss can only be reported by a pilot who believes that his/her aircraft may have been
endangered by the proximity of another aircraft (or, in regulated airspace, where an Air Traffic
Controller believes there has been a risk of collision). On both of these occasions an air
exclusion zone had been set up so that display aircraft could conduct manoeuvres knowing that
there would be no other aircraft in the area. An airmiss was not reported on either day because
the civilian aircraft did not get close to the display aircraft. However, as they had entered the
exclusion zone, the display leader thought it was unwise to continue and aborted the displays.

Details of these incidents were passed to the Civil Aviation Authority for any action they thought
necessary.

I hope this clarifies the matter.

Thank you for your letter of 19 May 2003 requesting information on a object you captured on your video

understand why it has taken you four years to bring this to our attention. As you are undoubtedly aware, after
this period of time most of the Service personnel on duty on that day have been posted throughout the wider
Royal Air Force and it is almost impossible to contact them all for their recollections of the day. Thatsaid,
nothing ‘untoward’ was brought to our attention on the day, or in the subsequent period, by any of the
approximately 45,000 visitors who attended the Air Show, many of whom were also “experienced air activity
observers’, nor the approximately 3,000 Service personnel who were on duty during that day. ‘herefore, this

civilian pilot also breached the air exclusion zone during an International Team’s display, which was
immediately cancelled on air safety grounds. Both civilian pilots were traced and censored by the CAA.

Exemption 2 of the Code

Exemption 2 of the Code

11. Exemption 2 protects,

“Information whose disclose would harm the frankness and candour of

However, it is not sufficient merely to determine that information falls within the
scope of Exemption 2. Even if this is the case ....

“the presumption remains that information should be disclosed unless the
harm likely to arise from disclosure would outweigh the public interest in
making the information available”

12. In previous rulings on the use of Exemption 2 the Ombudsman has viewed this

disclosure outweighed the general principle that the internal decision making process
should remain confidential. In that particular case the Ombudsman ordered that a
summary of the information (although not the documents themselves) be released.

18. A number of different lines could be taken in commenting to the Ombudsman.
All of the options presented, however, are based on the premise that we will need to be
open about the administrative errors that have now come to light. The Ombudsman must
be expected to comment adversely on the break-down of internal processes, but

acknowledgement of the mistakes and acceptance of the need to learn the relevant lessons
may take the sting out of this.

Option A would say that as some of these items have been inadvertently
released into the public domain, the remainder should also be released. This
would undoubtedly please Section 4G other applicants. The disadvantage
is that it would weaken the precedent that internal advice is not disclosed, and
it would be particularly difficult to use Exemption 2 in response to any other
requests by the UFO community. Indeed, it may prompt appeals to the
Ombudsman about previous refusals.

Option B would be to continue to disclose the items that were inadvertently
released, but to continue to withhold the others that have not. This would rely
on the argument that items that constitute internal advice were released by
mistake, and that although the error will not now be reversed it does not justify
a decision to knowingly release other internal advice. The difficulty here is
that one of the items that we would continue to withhold (the background note
to PQ 7607C) is identical to an item that would continue to be released.

Option C would be to argue that withholding the remaining items is justified
because they contain no new information. This argument is based on the
premise that ESSE asked for “information regarding the events of
December 1980 in and around Rendlesham’ and that the Code is not a right of
access to documents, merely to information. A strong case can be made that
in providing over 170 documents toffeiellew we have answered his request,
and it is certainly true that the remaining documents contain no new

substantive information. However, given that this was not the stated basis for
rejectingiete femppeal, this argument is not without flaws.

Option D would be to accept that some information has been inadvertently
released to some applicants, but to explain that we would not want to
perpetuate the error. In practice, to explain to the Ombudsman that we
propose to revert to the original intention about what information should be
withheld. Although this might lead to some future criticism from the
Ombudsman and the UFO community, it would have the advantage of being
consistent with the reasons the document were originally withheld, and with
the decision of the internal review (Enclosure 6). The argument would be that
not only do the documents contain no new substantive information but these
particular items constitute internal advice and are therefore subject to
Exemption 2 of the Code. The harm in releasing the documents would be that
it would endanger the candour of internal advice as it would remove the
presumption of confidentiality. Furthermore the fact that the documents
contain no new information could be deployed to demonstrate that there is no
public interest in releasing them.

Option E is a variant of D. This would follow the line set out above, but
would release the cover sheets to the two PQ submissions and the two letters
from external correspondents, as in isolation these do not constitute internal
advice.

19. The arguments are finely balanced but my recommendation is to follow
Option E. The merit is that this retains the principles on which MOD originally evaluated
the documents contained within the Rendlesham File, and under which the internal
reviews were subsequently carried out. The fact that some of the information was
mistakenly disclosed does not alter the principle that it was appropriate to withhold it.
Moreover, the documents withheld must be viewed in light of the 170 or so that have
been disclosed. This demonstrates that the Department has taken its obligation under the
Code seriously, and that the first instinct was to disclose, not to withhold information.
The fact that all substantive information has already been released to Section 4Caiantl
also demonstrate that there is no further public interest to be served by disclosing the few
documents that remain. It must be recognised, however, that the Ombudsman may well
see matters differently. For example he might take the view that as the information is
now some 17 years old and the documents released inadvertently are already in the public
domain, it is inappropriate to seek to maintain a principle that has already been breached.
Clearly we will have to wait and see and then consider our response in light of the
premise on which he bases any case for disclosure.

Option A would say that as some of these items have been inadvertently released into the

items that constitute internal advice were released by mistake, and that although the error
will not now be reversed it does not justify a decision to knowingly release other internal
advice. The difficulty here is that one of the items that we would continue to withhold

Option C would be to argue that withholding the remaining items is justified because they

PART B: SUMMARY OF THE WORKING GROUP’S DISCUSSIONS

Information available to the Working Group included reports from the pilot of the B737, transcri pts of
the relevant RT frequencies, a video recording, and reports from the uir traffic controllers involved.

The Group were anxious to emphasise that this report, submitted by two responsible airline pilots, was
considered seriously and they wished to commend the pilots for their courage in submitting it, and
their company, whose enlightened attitude made it possible. Reports such as these are often the abject
of derision, but the Group hopes that this example will encourage pilots who experience unusual
sightings Lo report them without fear of ridicule. It was quickly realised by all members that, because
of its unusual nature, they could only theorise on the possibilities once normal avenues of investigation
had been explored. There is no doubs that the pilots both saw an object and that it was of sufficient
Significance to pronipt an airmiss report, Unfortunately, the nature and identity of this object remains
unknown. To speculate about extra-terrestrial activity, fascinating though it may be, is not within the
Group's remit and must be left to those whose interest lies in that field. It is probably true to say,
however, that almose all unusual sightings can be attributed to 2 wide range of well known natural
